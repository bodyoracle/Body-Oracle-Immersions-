A Note About This Journal

Bring a notebook + pen.

This digital journal is meant to accompany you, not to be filled in on a screen. Keep a physical notebook nearby and let your responses move through your hand onto the page.

Handwriting asks us to slow down. The movement of hand and arm gives thought, sensation and feeling a physical pathway for expression, which can support attention, reflection and integration. Within this immersion, we also hold the simple act of putting pen to paper as part of the listening practice—a way of bringing what is internal into movement, form and relationship.

You do not need to answer every question. Follow what has energy. Leave what does not. Sometimes the most useful response is a sentence, a drawing, a few words—or closing the journal and going outside.

Before You Begin

The journal is part of the listening practice.

You do not need to analyze every experience or turn each moment into insight. Sometimes integration looks like writing. Sometimes it looks like eating, sleeping, walking, crying, laughing, sitting beside a tree, or doing absolutely nothing.

If a question or practice makes you feel more overwhelmed, disoriented, faint, panicked, or disconnected, stop. Orient to the room or land around you, find another person if you need support, and return to what feels simple and steady.

Seven Movements

Follow the arc, not the calendar.

You may move through these in order, circle back, or find several happening at once. Use whichever movement feels alive.

1. ARRIVE — What is here?

What do I notice as I enter this place? Where does my body feel open? Where does it brace, hurry, or hold back?

Land inquiry: What place on the land draws me toward it? What changes when I stay there for a few minutes without needing anything to happen?

2. REGULATE — What helps me settle?

What tells me I am becoming more present? Warmth? Hunger? A deeper breath? Tears? Fatigue? More sensation? More choice?

What supports me today: food, rest, movement, company, solitude, touch, quiet, water, nature?

3. LISTEN — What becomes audible when I stop pushing?

What have I been moving too quickly to hear?

What am I noticing in sensation before I turn it into a story?

4. RELATE — What happens between me and another?

When do I move toward? When do I pull away? What happens when I need something, disagree, feel misunderstood, or want to say no?

What does trust feel like in my body when it is actually present?

5. REMEMBER — What is older than this moment?

Did a memory, image, person, place, dream, lineage story, or younger version of me arise?

What gift do I recognize alongside the wound?

6. INTEGRATE — What wants to stay with me?

Of everything I have encountered, what feels essential rather than merely interesting?

What is one small practice or choice I genuinely want to continue?

7. RETURN — What am I taking home?

What do I understand differently about myself now?

What support, boundary, relationship, rhythm, or practice will help this remembering survive contact with ordinary life?

When Something Big Arises

Small doses. More choice. Return to the present.

You do not need to “get through” an intense feeling in one sitting. If something charged appears, work in a small enough dose that you can still notice the present moment around you.

ORIENT → RESOURCE → MOVE → WRITE → REST

1. Orient. Look around slowly. Name five things you can see. Notice where you are and what tells you this moment is different from the past.

2. Resource. Find one thing that feels neutral, pleasant, supportive, or steady: your feet, a tree, a blanket, someone's voice, the chair beneath you.

3. Move, if your body wants to. Press your feet into the floor, stretch, walk, gently shake out your hands and legs for 30–60 seconds, or make another small movement that feels organizing. Stop if movement increases distress.

4. Write for 3–5 minutes. Start with: “Right now I notice…” Stay close to present sensation rather than forcing the whole story.

5. Rest. Do something ordinary: drink water, eat, lie down, step outside, sit quietly, or connect with someone.

This is a simple trauma-informed integration practice created for this journal. It is not presented as Somatic Experiencing® instruction or as a substitute for trauma therapy.

Pendulation of Attention

You do not have to stare only at what hurts.

Notice one place in your body that feels difficult or charged. Stay only long enough to recognize it. Then intentionally notice somewhere that feels easier, neutral, supported, warm, spacious, or simply less intense.

Move your attention gently between the two. You are not trying to make the difficult sensation disappear. You are reminding your nervous system that more than one experience can exist at the same time.
